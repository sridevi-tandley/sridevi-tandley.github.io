Six steps. One evening. A phone is enough.

  1. Your fileStart from one document you already handle — here, a personal-loan application. Practice copies only; never a real applicant's.
  2. CreateOpen a free agent builder — no code, no installation — and create a new agent.
  3. InstructWrite the four blocks below: who it helps, the steps it follows, the shape of its output, and the rules it may never break.
  4. Attach & checkAttach the file and read back what the agent extracted. Verify what it read before you trust what it makes.
  5. RunLet it fill the Pre-Check Card: documents present or missing, score against the threshold, and a recommendation — never a decision.
  6. YoursIt is now your agent. Build it, break it, fix it — and keep the human sign-off exactly where it was.

Key insights from the masterclass

Agents do; assistants answer

A chatbot replies. An agent plans, calls tools, checks its own result and acts — inside limits a person has written down. That difference is the whole subject of the day.

Saved hours are not value

Four hours a week saved are reabsorbed by default — meetings expand, work stretches. Redeploying the hours is a decision someone has to take; if nobody takes it, the default wins.

The reversibility test

If an action cannot be undone — money moved, a customer told, a loan approved — a human signs. That is not a regulator's request; it is correct design.

Shadow → Assisted → Sampled → Autonomous

Agents earn autonomy in stages: run alongside people first, then assist, then be sampled and audited, and only then act alone on the narrowest task that has proved safe.

Confidence-based escalation

An agent that answers a trivial refund question and a frightening fraud question with identical confidence is mis-designed. Route by uncertainty and by stakes, not by topic alone.

Design it. Build it. Break it. Value it.

Every team writes a spec sheet on paper, builds in a cluster of three or four around one laptop, breaks it on purpose — including a prompt-injection attempt — and then prices what it is worth.

The growth ladder — what an agent is worth

  • L1
    Same output, fewer hoursCost saving — and where most organisations stop.
  • L2
    More output, same hoursRevenue grows without headcount.
  • L3
    Work you could not attempt beforeNew capability, new lines of business.
  • L4
    Cost-to-serve falls enough to open closed marketsCustomers and segments that were never viable become viable.

Productivity gains compete away — everyone gets the same capability at the same price, so L1 and L2 advantages are temporary. Durable advantage still lives in proprietary data, workflow depth, distribution and trust. The agent is leverage on your advantage, not the advantage.

Why agent projects fail — and what the day teaches instead

  • Escalating cost → bounded loops. Twelve iterations are twelve round trips; "make everything an agent" is bad engineering.
  • Unclear business value → the growth ladder and the redeployment question, answered before anything is built.
  • Weak risk controls → reversibility, least privilege and an audit trail, taught inside the technical material rather than as a separate ethics module.

How the room works

  • Every concept runs in two lanes — a sixty-second plain-language anchor for everyone, then the mechanism at full technical depth, clearly labelled optional.
  • Pairs and clusters mix technical and non-technical participants on purpose: the domain person owns the specification and judges the output; the builder types. The person who catches the problem is almost never the person typing.
  • The showcase asks four questions of every team: what does it do, what broke, what is it worth in hours or rupees and at which growth level, and what would move it up a level.
  • Everyone leaves with a complete build kit — the spec sheet, a take-home build guide and this companion film — so the first agent can be finished at home the same evening.
